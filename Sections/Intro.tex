\section{Introduction}

Autoregressive generation in large language models (LLMs) is fundamentally
memory-bandwidth bound, making inference at scale exceptionally costly. To
mitigate this, lossless acceleration paradigms---most notably speculative
decoding \citep{leviathan2023speculative}---have been rapidly adopted across
modern LLM serving stacks. By pairing a smaller draft model with a larger
target verifier, speculative decoding achieves substantial speedups without
altering the final output distribution. However, this guarantee of semantic
equivalence masks a severe operational vulnerability: the mechanism remains
efficient only when user prompts organically induce high token-level alignment
between the draft and target models.

This assumption is fragile. Once draft-target agreement deteriorates, drafted
blocks are rejected, verifier intervention increases, and the system is forced
back toward its worst-case sequential regime. Most prior work studies this
acceptance bottleneck from the defensive side by improving draft-target
alignment, redesigning verification, or adapting draft length online
\citep{liu2023online,goel2024direct,agrawal2024adaedl,bachmann2025judge,wang2025alignment,zhou2026hsd}.
We study the complementary adversarial question: can a short prompt prefix
erase the efficiency gains of speculative decoding while preserving the
user-visible task outcome?

This question sits within a broader shift in the security landscape of machine
learning systems. Adversaries increasingly target system availability and
inference cost rather than semantic correctness alone. Sponge examples showed
that carefully crafted inputs can drive neural networks toward worst-case
energy-latency behavior \citep{shumailov2021sponge}. More recently, Engorgio
demonstrated that autoregressive LLMs can be coerced into abnormally long
generations, inflating compute cost and inducing denial-of-service style
behavior \citep{dong2024engorgio}. Yet such attacks are often conspicuous
because they fundamentally alter output length or visibly degrade response
quality.

Speculative decoding creates a stealthier attack surface. An attacker need not
force repetitive loops or degenerate text. Instead, a short adversarial prefix
can directly target the draft-target verification mechanism and deliberately
collapse verifier acceptance. When draft proposals are repeatedly rejected,
the system discards the speculative speedup and reverts toward worst-case
sequential execution. For cloud providers, this degradation functions as a
denial-of-wallet attack that inflates per-request compute costs and threatens
latency service-level objectives. For edge deployments, it becomes a silent
energy-exhaustion attack, even while the final response remains semantically
benign to standard safety filters.

The primary technical obstacle is the continuous-to-discrete optimization gap.
The natural attack objective---maximizing draft-target disagreement---is most
amenable to gradient-based search in a continuous embedding space. Deployment,
however, requires a discrete text string. Naive continuous optimization can
therefore discover powerful relaxed adversarial states that fail
catastrophically when projected back to valid tokens. In this work, we elevate
that projection gap to a first-class object of study and introduce ADSD, a
principled framework for prompt-side acceptance collapse. Rather than relying
on heuristic discrete search alone, ADSD explicitly controls projection
distortion and constructs deployable adversarial prefixes that survive the
transition from continuous optimization to real text.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item We formulate prompt-side speculative slowdown as a
continuous-to-discrete optimization problem, prove a prompt-waywardness
impossibility result, and derive a relaxation-gap bound that justifies
explicit projection-distortion control.
\item We introduce ADSD, a verifier-aligned framework for prompt-side
acceptance collapse that optimizes adversarial prefixes while respecting the
discrete deployment constraint.
\item We show empirically that prompt-only attacks can materially degrade
speculative efficiency while preserving user-visible task quality, exposing
lossless acceleration as a prompt-controlled inference-cost surface.
\end{itemize}

The broader message is simple. Lossless output semantics do not imply cost
robustness. A short user-controlled prefix can leave the final answer intact
while forcing the verifier to do substantially more work.
