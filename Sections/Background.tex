\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

Speculative decoding accelerates autoregressive generation by pairing a small
draft model with a large target verifier \citep{leviathan2023speculative}. Let
$q_i(\cdot \mid h_i)$ denote the draft distribution at speculative step $i$
given history $h_i$, and let $p_i(\cdot \mid h_i)$ denote the corresponding
target distribution.

The verifier either commits the drafted token or replaces it with a
target-sampled correction. Consequently, speculative efficiency depends
directly on the overlap between $p_i$ and $q_i$: high agreement yields long
accepted draft blocks, whereas low agreement forces frequent verifier
intervention.

This dependence has an especially important consequence for our setting:
one-step verifier acceptance decreases as the draft and target distributions
diverge. That link makes the attack surface explicit. Any prompt that
increases early draft-target disagreement will, in expectation, reduce
verifier acceptance and therefore diminish speculative speedup. At the same
time, it also reveals the methodological obstacle that
drives the rest of the paper: maximizing verifier disagreement over a deployed
prompt requires optimization over discrete tokens, whereas the most informative
search signals live in a continuous relaxation. The attack problem is
therefore not just to increase TV, but to do so in a way that survives
projection back to text.

The systems metrics used in the evaluation section arise from this same
acceptance process. Aggregate acceptance captures how much draft computation
survives verification, while block efficiency measures how much useful drafted
work is recovered per verifier step. We defer the formal metric definitions to
Section~\ref{sec:eval}, where they are introduced alongside the experimental
protocol.

\subsection{Related Work}

\paragraph{Speculative decoding under benign prompt assumptions.}
The speculative-decoding literature overwhelmingly treats low acceptance as an
efficiency problem to be repaired. Alignment-centric approaches adapt or train
the draft model so that its token proposals more closely match the target
\citep{liu2023online,goel2024direct,wang2025alignment}. Verification-centric
approaches instead redesign the accept-reject mechanism itself, for example by
predicting likely failures and halting drafting adaptively, or by recovering
useful work even under partial disagreement
\citep{agrawal2024adaedl,bachmann2025judge,zhou2026hsd}. These methods differ
substantially in mechanism, but they share the same premise: prompts are
treated as benign, and the system designer's goal is to preserve acceptance
under ordinary inputs. Our work begins exactly where that premise breaks down.

\paragraph{Prompt optimization as an attack primitive.}
Outside speculative decoding, adversarial NLP has shown that short optimized
token sequences can manipulate model behavior in highly transferable ways.
Universal adversarial triggers and suffixes demonstrate that a single
input-agnostic string can induce systematic failures across many examples
\citep{wallace2019uat,zou2023universal}. Evolutionary and discrete search
methods such as AutoDAN further show that effective prompts need not resemble
natural language \citep{liu2023autodan}. However, these attacks primarily
target semantic behavior: they seek harmful, policy-violating, or incorrect
outputs. ADSD instead targets the verification dynamics of the serving
mechanism itself. The central object of manipulation is not the meaning of the
response, but the efficiency of the draft-target interaction that produces it.

\paragraph{Inference-cost and availability attacks.}
The security literature has also shifted toward attacks that inflate latency,
energy, or compute cost rather than model error alone. Sponge examples show
that carefully constructed inputs can force neural networks toward worst-case
energy-latency behavior \citep{shumailov2021sponge}. In autoregressive LLMs,
Engorgio achieves a related goal by suppressing \texttt{<EOS>} and inducing
abnormally long outputs, thereby increasing per-request inference cost
\citep{dong2024engorgio}. ADSD belongs to this availability-oriented family,
but its mechanism is substantially different. Rather than elongating or
degenerating the output, it attacks the acceptance process inside speculative
decoding itself. This makes the degradation both more targeted and potentially
stealthier: the speedup can disappear even when the final answer remains
semantically benign.

Taken together, these lines of work expose a clear gap. Prior speculative
decoding research assumes benign prompts and tries to maximize acceptance;
prior prompt attacks mostly target semantics; and prior availability attacks do
not study the verifier structure of speculative decoding. ADSD is designed for
precisely that missing setting.
