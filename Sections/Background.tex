\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

Speculative decoding accelerates autoregressive generation by pairing a small
draft model with a large target verifier \citep{leviathan2023speculative}. Let
$q_i(\cdot \mid h_i)$ denote the draft distribution at speculative step $i$
given history $h_i$, and let $p_i(\cdot \mid h_i)$ denote the corresponding
target distribution.

The verifier either commits the drafted token or replaces it with a
target-sampled correction. Consequently, speculative efficiency depends
directly on the overlap between $p_i$ and $q_i$: high agreement yields long
accepted draft blocks, whereas low agreement forces frequent verifier
intervention.

This dependence has an especially important consequence for our setting:
one-step verifier acceptance decreases as the draft and target distributions
diverge. That link makes the attack surface explicit. Any prompt that
increases early draft-target disagreement will, in expectation, reduce
verifier acceptance and therefore diminish speculative speedup. At the same
time, it also reveals the methodological obstacle that
drives the rest of the paper: maximizing verifier disagreement over a deployed
prompt requires optimization over discrete tokens, whereas the most informative
search signals live in a continuous relaxation. The attack problem is
therefore not just to increase TV, but to do so in a way that survives
projection back to text.

The systems metrics used in the evaluation section arise from this same
acceptance process. Aggregate acceptance captures how much draft computation
survives verification, while block efficiency measures how much useful drafted
work is recovered per verifier step. We defer the formal metric definitions to
Section~\ref{sec:eval}, where they are introduced alongside the experimental
protocol.

\subsection{Related Work}

\paragraph{Alignment-centric speculative enhancements.}
One major line of work treats low acceptance as a model-alignment problem.
Online Speculative Decoding adapts the draft model to the observed query
distribution \citep{liu2023online}. Direct alignment methods train the draft
to better match the target on instruction-following behavior
\citep{goel2024direct}, and related alignment-aware designs further improve
acceptance by narrowing the draft-target discrepancy under benign workloads
\citep{wang2025alignment}. The unifying assumption in this line is that poor
acceptance reflects an incidental mismatch to be corrected so that speculative
speedup is restored.

\paragraph{Verification-centric speculative enhancements.}
A second line of work treats low acceptance as a verification problem rather
than purely a draft-training problem. AdaEDL predicts when acceptance is
likely to fail and shortens drafting adaptively \citep{agrawal2024adaedl}.
Judge Decoding redesigns acceptance logic beyond simple model agreement
\citep{bachmann2025judge}, and HSD enlarges the verifier design space by
recovering useful work even when the draft and target do not align perfectly
\citep{zhou2026hsd}. These systems are more flexible than alignment-only
approaches, but they still study speculative decoding under the default
assumption of benign, in-distribution inputs.

Taken together, both paradigms seek to preserve acceptance under ordinary
serving conditions. That leaves a common blind spot: neither line asks whether
an adversary can intentionally manipulate prompt text to collapse acceptance
and convert speculative acceleration into additional verifier work. ADSD is
designed precisely for that setting.

\paragraph{Adversarial prompt optimization.}
Outside speculative decoding, adversarial NLP has shown that short optimized
token sequences can reliably manipulate model behavior. Universal adversarial
triggers demonstrate that input-agnostic token strings can induce systematic
failures across many examples \citep{wallace2019uat}. In aligned-chat settings,
universal suffix attacks and evolutionary jailbreak search methods such as
AutoDAN show that discrete prompt optimization can steer models toward harmful
semantic outputs \citep{zou2023universal,liu2023autodan}. Those methods are
well suited to output manipulation, where the goal is to change the semantic
content of the response. Our setting is different: we optimize prompts to alter
the \emph{systems behavior} of speculative verification itself. The target
answer can remain correct while the verifier is forced to do substantially more
work.

\paragraph{Prompt-based inference-cost attacks.}
The closest attack-style prior art is Engorgio, which constructs prompts that
suppress \texttt{<EOS>} and induce abnormally long outputs in ordinary
autoregressive decoding \citep{dong2024engorgio}. Our work is related in
spirit but differs in mechanism. Engorgio attacks output length in standard
decoding. ADSD attacks the acceptance process in speculative decoding by making
draft proposals hard for the verifier to accept.
