\section{Introduction}

Lossless acceleration has become one of the central systems ideas in modern
large language model (LLM) serving. Among these methods, speculative decoding
is especially attractive because it promises a rare combination: substantial
speedup without changing the target model's output distribution
\citep{leviathan2023speculative}. That guarantee, however, is semantic rather
than operational. It says that the final answer distribution is preserved; it
does not say that the serving stack is robust to adversarial inputs that
increase the amount of verifier work required to realize that distribution.

This distinction exposes a natural systems vulnerability. Speculative decoding
achieves speedup only when a small draft model remains well aligned with a
larger target verifier. When that alignment breaks down, drafted blocks are
rejected, verifier intervention increases, and the expected latency gains can
evaporate. Most prior work studies this acceptance bottleneck from the
defensive side by improving draft-target alignment, modifying verification, or
adapting draft length online
\citep{liu2023online,goel2024direct,agrawal2024adaedl,bachmann2025judge,wang2025alignment,zhou2026hsd}.
We study the complementary adversarial question: can a short prompt prefix
erase the efficiency gains of speculative decoding while preserving the
user-visible task outcome?

This attack surface is practical in deployment. The attacker does not need
custom scheduler privileges or parameter access at inference time. Instead, the
attacker controls only prompt text. Because speculative throughput depends
directly on draft-target agreement, prompt-side inputs can act as systems-level
levers: they can induce draft behavior that the target repeatedly corrects,
thereby converting speculative acceleration into additional verifier work.

The technical obstacle is discrete. Gradient-based prompt search is naturally
continuous, but deployment requires a discrete text string. In practice, this
means that naive continuous optimization can discover a strong relaxed attack
that fails after projection back to tokens. Our central methodological claim is
that this continuous-to-discrete projection gap must be treated as a first-class
object. We therefore develop a theoretically bounded optimizer that
explicitly penalizes projection distortion and ranks retained attacks by their
behavior in the actual verifier pipeline.

On the algorithmic side, we instantiate this idea in two stages. The first is
a verifier-aligned soft-prefix attack that targets early verifier collapse at
the assistant boundary. The second strengthens the attack into a
calibration-batch universal prefix optimized across multiple prompts and
multiple timestamps, with a gradient-guided discrete seed-search stage that
improves the quality of the final projected attack.

We evaluate ADSD in the reproduced HSD workspace using
\texttt{Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct-GPTQ-Int8} as the draft model and
\texttt{Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct-GPTQ-Int8} as the target model. On a matched
263-sample token-wise GSM8K benchmark, the initial boundary attack raises mean
sample time from $23.10$s to $29.81$s while reducing aggregate acceptance from
$0.597$ to $0.509$ and block efficiency from $5.832$ to $4.961$. The stronger
calibration-batch universal attack raises mean sample time to $38.61$s, lowers
aggregate acceptance to $0.390$, lowers block efficiency to $3.731$, and
reduces decoding speed to $51.68$, while matching the benign token-wise GSM8K
accuracy exactly at $222/263$.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Theory.} We formulate prompt-side speculative slowdown as a
continuous-to-discrete optimization problem, prove a prompt-waywardness
impossibility result, and derive a relaxation-gap bound that justifies
explicit projection-distortion control.
\item \textbf{Algorithm.} We introduce ADSD, a verifier-aligned attack that
combines bounded continuous optimization with discrete projection-aware model
selection, and extend it to a calibration-batch universal-prefix pipeline with
gradient-guided seed search.
\item \textbf{Empirical results.} On a reproduced HSD/Qwen stack, we show that
prompt-only attacks can materially degrade speculative efficiency, and that the
strongest accuracy-preserving universal prefix raises end-to-end runtime by
$67.1\%$ on a matched token-wise benchmark.
\end{itemize}

The broader message is simple. Lossless output semantics do not imply cost
robustness. A short user-controlled prefix can leave the final answer intact
while forcing the verifier to do substantially more work.
